1. What the paper does

The paper names two failure modes of CBMs that score concepts with a vision–language model (CLIP, DINO):

  • Semantic inconsistency. A part–attribute concept is scored in the wrong place on the object, e.g. a head attribute lights up the body.
  • Object inconsistency. An object-agnostic concept fires on the wrong object, e.g. "head: streamlined front profile" activates on a person standing next to the fish.

Their model, OA-CBM, addresses these with three ingredients:

  1. Part–attribute concepts. An LLM first lists each class's parts, then writes attributes for each part, e.g. "head: tubular nostrils atop the bill". Near-duplicates above 0.9 similarity are removed (SemHash). The top-K concepts are then kept by mutual information between VLM scores and LLM caption labels.
  2. Two grounding datasets. PartAttrCUB is built on the PartCUB-70 masks, with 8 parts: head, eye, beak, neck, body, wing, leg and tail. PartAttrImageNet is the second. Within each part, species that look alike are merged into clusters, and each cluster gets 3–5 attributes.
  3. Two learned modules. Cost aggregation (CA, in the style of CAT-Seg, trained with mask supervision) reduces the wrong-place problem. Hierarchical clustering (HC) builds a foreground object mask that is multiplied into the concept maps before pooling. The CLIP text encoder is also replaced by Llama3 (LLM2CLIP).

They also propose an evaluation task, Open Concept Grounding, with three protocols: Oracle-Obj (the true object mask and class are given), Oracle-All (only the true object mask is given) and Pred-All (nothing is given).

2. Feedback: what it does and does not show

The core problem is that the paper changes five things at once between a baseline VLM-CBM and OA-CBM, but ablates only two of them.

ClaimEvidenceAssessment
Finer-grained annotations drive the grounding gains ("thanks to our efforts on finer-grained annotations") On PartAttrCUB, Pred-All h-IoU rises from 4.4 (best baseline) to 20.8 not isolated No ablation keeps the model fixed and changes only the concept format or the annotations. CA is trained on the new dataset, so dataset and module are entangled, and the text-encoder swap is a third confound.
Object masking matters Under Pred-All on PartAttrCUB, adding the object mask lifts h-IoU from 6.6 to 20.8 well isolated The cleanest result in the paper (Table 1).
Better grounding gives better patch-level classification With CA, CUB patch-token accuracy rises from 33.8 to 63.2 (Table 7) plausible But HC without CA scores 0.01% on CUB and 0.1% on FOOD, which looks like a collapsed training run rather than a real result.
Patch-based scoring is less accurate than CLS-based CUB: 80.7 (CLS token) vs 63.3 (patch token) consistent The same interpretability vs accuracy trade-off appears in this repo's SEG-MIL work.
Open questions for the authors' setup.
  • Neither the paper nor the supplement says whether the PartCUB-70 images used to train CA/HC overlap the CUB-200 test split used for classification. Check this before comparing numbers.
  • PartAttrCUB labels are assigned per cluster of species, not per image. "Data quality" here means a better vocabulary plus part masks; the per-image correctness of labels is not addressed.
The takeaway for us. The paper suggests that data and concept design matter a lot, but its ablations cannot separate data from modules. That separation is exactly what a controlled design study like this repo can provide, and it is the gap a paper could fill.

3. What it means for this codebase

What the CUB loader currently discards

  • Annotator certainty. CUB records a certainty for every label (2 = guessing, 3 = probably, 4 = definitely). The loader uses only "1 = not visible", and only during majority voting.
  • Per-image labels. With class_level_concepts=True, every image gets its class's majority-voted vector.
  • Part visibility. The concept loss trains on positives whose part is hidden.
  • A coarse attribute-to-part mapping. malar, eyebrow, eyeline and masked all map to crown/forehead (issue #1).

4. Choosing a paper: improve the data, or measure its effect?

A. Effect of data (measurement study)B. Improving the datasets (method or dataset paper)
Core questionWhich properties of the training data change what a CBM learns, and by how much? Does a cleaned or relabelled dataset give better CBMs?
ContributionControlled effect sizes for each data factor, a public grid of results and the audit toolkit A new or cleaned dataset (e.g. a re-annotated CUB) plus evidence that it helps
Fit with the repoVery high. The repo already exists to vary one factor at a time, across seeds, with a mixed-effects analysis. Medium. Needs annotation work, human or VLM, and a release.
Main riskFindings that are "obvious" unless the effect sizes or interactions are surprising Annotation cost; reviewers asking whether the gains come from the data or from the cleaning model
Possible venuesMain-track ML/CV conferences (analysis papers), TMLR Datasets & benchmarks tracks
Recommendation: start with A, and let it lead into B. Measure first: which data levers move accuracy, intervention gains, leakage and localization? Then fix only the most damaging lever and show the gain. That gives the paper a story ("measure, then fix") and keeps the cleaning step honest, because it is justified by the measurements. A working title: "It's the Data: A Controlled Study of How Annotations Shape Concept Bottleneck Models."

5. Study design

Research questions

  1. RQ1, label quality. How do concept-label noise, granularity (per image or per class) and annotator certainty affect task accuracy, concept accuracy and intervention gains?
  2. RQ2, concept vocabulary. At a fixed model, does the concept format matter: human attributes, object-level LLM concepts, part–attribute LLM concepts or SAE concepts? This is the ablation the OA-CBM paper does not run.
  3. RQ3, spatial supervision. What do keypoints, part masks or object masks add to grounding, and does better grounding transfer to accuracy or robustness?
  4. RQ4, annotation budget. How many concept-labelled images are needed? Where does the curve flatten?
  5. RQ5, interactions. Does the backbone or training scheme (sequential, independent, joint) change how sensitive a CBM is to data quality?

Hypotheses (to test, not claims)

  • H1. Concept-label noise hurts intervention gains more than task accuracy: the head learns to route around unreliable concepts, which shows up as more leakage.
  • H2. Per-class labels raise measured concept accuracy but lower localization, because they teach "species identity" rather than "visible attribute".
  • H3. Part–attribute vocabularies improve localization clearly but accuracy only a little.
  • H4. Object masking lowers reliance on the background, raising worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds.

Vary one factor at a time from a fixed anchor (e.g. cub_human), and add a small full grid only for the interactions you care about (RQ5). Use at least 3 seeds, and 5 for headline numbers.

Measures (dependent variables)

  • Accuracy: task accuracy, concept accuracy and F1 (concepts).
  • Reliance on concepts: intervention curves, the soft–hard gap, and a probe for the spurious attribute (leakage).
  • Grounding: part_iou, keypoint_distance, localization, matched by part once module 1 exists.
  • Robustness: worst-group and effective robustness on Waterbirds / MetaShift (shift).
  • Cost: annotation budget, so results can be read as Pareto frontiers.

Analysis

Use the existing mixed-effects models (analysis.effects) for each factor's effect with confidence intervals, Pareto frontiers of accuracy vs grounding vs annotation cost, and dose–response curves for noise and budget. Add the audit-vs-result join: does a concept's audit score (e.g. learnability) predict its grounding and its intervention value?

Figures the paper would need

  1. A dose–response plot: label-noise rate vs accuracy and intervention gain (the H1 test).
  2. A forest plot of effect sizes for every data factor, one panel per metric.
  3. A vocabulary × object-mask grid on CUB70: accuracy vs part IoU (the experiment OA-CBM does not run).
  4. An annotation-budget curve with Pareto frontiers.
  5. Qualitative concept maps for low- and high-quality concepts, taken from the audit.

7. An annotation platform that fits on a laptop

Annotation has a heavy part and a valuable part. The heavy part is the pixels: CUB alone is about 1.1 GB and ImageNet over 100 GB. The valuable part is the labels, which are tiny: one judgment is about 150 bytes. So the platform should keep pixels remote and send only what is on screen, while keeping labels local, small and versioned.

Five design principles

  1. Annotate only where it matters. Tasks come from the audits (§3): images where the probe disagrees with the label, guessed labels, positives with a hidden part. Never the whole dataset.
  2. Verify instead of labelling from scratch. One attribute, many part crops per screen, with the current label and the model's score shown. You only act on the tiles that are wrong. This is the fastest task type there is.
  3. Pixels stay remote. Crops are cut on Modal from the volume that already holds CUB (cbm-koh2020). They are downloaded per batch and deleted afterwards, or streamed through a cache with a fixed size limit.
  4. Labels are local and immutable once released. SQLite while you work. When you are done, an export to a JSONL snapshot with the content hash in its filename, committed to git.
  5. Measure the annotator too. Hidden gold items and repeated items give the self-agreement and inter-annotator numbers that a data paper must report.

How labels get back into experiments

One row per judgment, in the same terms as CUB's own certainty scale, so the loader can treat human and original labels the same way:

{"image_id": 1234, "part": "head", "attribute": "has_head_pattern::malar",
 "label": "absent", "certainty": 4, "annotator": "a1", "task": "malar-probe-disagree",
 "gold": false, "ts": "2026-10-20T14:03:11Z", "tool": "0.1.0"}
  • The dataset reads it through an opt-in kwarg: dataset: {name: cub, relabel: labels/cub-relabel-3f9a12c0.jsonl}. The default, None, keeps every existing result valid.
  • The cache key includes the file's hash, not just its path, and the key is added to tests/test_cache_keys.py. The run id only hashes the config, so an edited file under the same path would otherwise silently reuse old results. Hence the immutable, hash-named snapshots.
  • The effect of the relabelling is an ablation factor: original vs relabelled, everything else fixed. That is framing B from §4 (improving the data), measured the same way as framing A.
  • The task export (cbm-eval annotate export) is a new CLI command next to the audits. Like the audit registry, it is worth a short design issue first.

8. Risks and threats to validity

  • Reproducibility. Every data lever must be a new kwarg with the old behaviour as default. Changing defaults in place silently mixes old and new results under the same run id.
  • CUB peculiarities. Majority voting, the 112-concept filter and the 70-class mask subset may not generalize. Repeat the key results on a second dataset (AwA2 or CelebA are open issues #33 and #32).
  • Injected noise is not real noise. Uniform flips are easier than structured annotator errors. Also report effects of the real certainty labels.
  • LLM vocabularies drift. Cache discovery outputs (the repo's JsonCache) and publish them, so the vocabulary is fixed across runs.
  • Confounding by vocabulary size. Match the number of concepts K when comparing vocabularies.
  • Train/test overlap between part-mask training data and the classification test split (the open question about OA-CBM applies to us too).
